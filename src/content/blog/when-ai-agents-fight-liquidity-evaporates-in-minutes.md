Automated markets are no longer just for humans. They are now battlefields for autonomous agents, and the casualty rate is higher than most traders realize.

A new paper published on arXiv, [Agentic AI in Automated Markets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22522), reveals that heterogeneous agent behavior—where different AI models compete—creates severe stress, volatility spikes, and liquidity crunches. This isn't theoretical. The mechanics are already live in [automated market makers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22522) powering the majority of on-chain volume.

We model trading not as a game of prediction, but as a game of survival. When AI agents dominate the order book, the market stops reflecting human sentiment and starts reflecting algorithmic friction.

## The Three Archetypes of AI Stress

The study models three distinct behavioral archetypes using agentic reinforcement learning (RL). These aren't abstract concepts. They are the actual bots executing trades on your behalf or against you.

The first archetype is the trend-follower. It chases momentum until the liquidity dries up. The second is the mean-reverter. It bets against the move until the move becomes permanent. The third is the liquidity provider. It sets the spreads until the volatility eats its capital.

When these agents collide, the results are predictable. The paper documents how "stress events" emerge not from bad news, but from the interaction of these strategies. Liquidity vanishes precisely when you need it most—during the initial phase of a trend reversal.

This is why your stop-loss often fills below market price. The liquidity wasn't there. The AI agents had already pulled their orders to preserve capital.

## Volatility is a Feature, Not a Bug

The study highlights that risk-adjusted performance for individual agents often deteriorates during high-stress periods. This is because the market microstructure changes. Spreads widen. Depth disappears. The "random walk" assumption fails because the walk is being directed by competing algorithms.

For traders, this means the old rules of engagement are obsolete. You cannot rely on static support and resistance levels when the liquidity providers are dynamic, learning agents. They adapt faster than you can click.

The paper notes that "volatility clustering" is a direct result of these interactions. One agent's exit triggers another's liquidation, which triggers a cascade. This is the definition of a flash crash. It's not a glitch. It's the system working as designed.
