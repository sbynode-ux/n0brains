Bitcoin’s price drop has widened the gap between market value and the cost to produce it, according to [JPMorgan analysts](https://www.theblock.co/news/markets/2026-11-20-jpmorgan-bitcoin-price-correction-gap-86000-production-cost-444763). The bank estimates the production cost at **$86,000**. When the price falls below that threshold, miners operate at a loss. This isn’t just a balance sheet issue. It is a structural supply shock waiting to happen.

We’ve seen this movie before. When profitability collapses, hash rate drops. When hash rate drops, security metrics look weak. When security looks weak, institutional capital gets nervous. The current correction is not a random noise event. It is a stress test of the entire mining ecosystem.

JPMorgan notes that the price drop puts [pressure on miners](https://www.theblock.co/news/markets/2026-11-20-jpmorgan-bitcoin-price-correction-gap-86000-production-cost-444763). This pressure is immediate. Miners with high energy costs or older hardware are the first to flip off their rigs. They cannot sustain operations when the reward per terahash no longer covers electricity and depreciation. This is a natural market correction, but it is brutal for those who ignored the margin requirements.

The market is currently ignoring the supply side dynamics. Traders are focused on short-term price action, but the infrastructure layer is cracking. JPMorgan’s analysis suggests that the current price level is unsustainable for the marginal miner. If the price stays below **$86,000** for more than a few weeks, we will see a significant reduction in network hashrate. This could trigger a feedback loop: lower security leads to lower confidence, which leads to lower prices, which leads to more hash rate shutdowns.

The market is pricing in the price, but not the production cost.

JPMorgan’s estimate of **$86,000** is a baseline. Actual costs vary by region, energy source, and hardware efficiency. Miners in Texas with cheap renewable energy have a lower break-even than those in regions with expensive grid power. This disparity means that the first wave of hash rate shutdowns will come from the least efficient operators. The network will remain secure, but the total hashrate will decline. This is a feature, not a bug. It ensures that only the most efficient producers remain.

The market is currently pricing in a scenario where Bitcoin remains above **$86,000** indefinitely. If that assumption breaks, the consequences could be severe. JPMorgan’s report serves as a warning.
